# You Just Fit the Known Facts
### A talking-head video script (the untunability argument)

**Author:** Allen Proxmire
**Series:** ED Readings — the spoken version of the capstone (`ED_Reading_ReproducedFromThirteen_TheUntunabilityArgument.md`).
**Format:** ~7 minutes, first person, to camera. Written to be *read aloud*. `[pause]` marks a beat. Delivery: clear, a little dry, respectful of the skeptic. The whole credibility of this one is that you concede honestly and never overclaim, so don't rush the honest parts.

---

If you ever build a theory that explains a lot of things, the first thing a physicist says to you is not "wow." [pause] It's: "sure, but you just fit what we already knew."

I want to take that objection completely seriously, because it's the right objection. And then I want to show you why, in the cases that matter, it's weaker than it sounds.

Start with the rule everybody agrees on. A new theory has to do two things. It has to reproduce what the old theory got right, and it has to predict something new that we can go check. Reproduce, plus predict. That's the deal, and it's a good deal.

But here's the trap nobody mentions. If the old theory is already 99 percent right everywhere we can look, then the *new* theory has almost nowhere left to out-predict it. All the reachable experiments are already covered. The only places a new theory could say something different are the places we *can't* test yet. So a new theory that might genuinely be deeper gets stuck looking like it has nothing to offer, because the one move it's allowed to make, a fresh testable prediction, has almost no open ground left to make it on.

Which throws all the weight onto the other half of the deal: reproducing what we already know. And that's where the objection lands. "You just fit the known facts." [pause]

Now, let me give that objection its due, because there's a real worry underneath it. The worry is *overfitting*. If a theory is flexible enough, if it has enough knobs to turn, you can tune it to match anything. And a theory that can match anything has told you nothing. So when someone reproduces a known result, the honest skeptic is asking: did you *tune* your way to that, after the fact? A brand-new prediction can't be tuned, because the fact didn't exist yet when you built the theory. That's why new predictions feel airtight.

Here's the thing they get wrong. [pause] "Known versus new" is not actually the line that matters. It's a stand-in for the real question, and the real question is: *could you have tuned it?*

Because a new prediction isn't special because it's new. It's special because you couldn't have cheated toward it. And it turns out there's *another* way to not be able to cheat: your theory can be too small to tune. If the structure is rigid enough that fitting it to one fact forces what it says about a completely different fact, then you couldn't have fitted your way to both. The match is real. Old fact or new, it counts exactly the same.

So the question was never *when* did we learn the fact. It was always: *did your theory have enough room to cheat?*

Let me make that concrete, because it sounds abstract until you see it. [pause]

The cleanest example is a hundred years old, and it's the bending of starlight. Take any theory of gravity, and fit it to the everyday stuff, falling apples, the orbits of the planets. When you do that, you've set how your theory warps *time*. But the same structure that warps time also warps *space*, and you don't get to choose that part separately. It comes along for the ride. And when you carry it through, the bending of starlight as it passes the sun is forced to be exactly *twice* what you'd naively guess from Newton. Not a little more. Double. [pause] You cannot fit the falling apple and then turn a dial to fix the starlight. One is locked to the other. Eddington went and measured it in 1919, and it was the double. That's untunability you can point at. Nobody drew that backwards from the answer, because you can't.

Here's one from my own corner of this. There's a scale where galaxies stop behaving the way they should, the thing people usually explain with dark matter. In the framework I work on, that scale isn't a fitted number. It falls out as the speed of light times the expansion rate of the universe, divided by two pi. That's it. No adjustable coefficient. And it lands within about ten percent of the measured value. [pause] There's no knob to turn there. You either get that number out of the structure, or you don't. It got it.

Now let me be honest about what I am *not* saying, because this is exactly where people like me oversell, and I'm not going to. [pause]

I am not telling you this framework out-predicts Einstein or quantum mechanics where we can already test. It mostly doesn't. There's barely any room there, and pretending otherwise would be a lie. And I am *not* telling you it derives all the numbers from nothing. Most of the constants are still borrowed from experiment. What it gives you is the *shape* of things, the *form* of the law, not the value.

So the claim is narrower than "it explains everything," and narrower is what makes it honest. The claim is: one small structure gives the shape of a lot of *unrelated* things, gravity, and quantum probability, and the behavior of galaxies, and it is too small to have been quietly fitted to all of them one at a time. That's not proof. It has a name, actually. It's called consilience, and it persuades, but it doesn't force. The forcing would come from a new confirmed prediction, and I think there's one worth betting on. But that's a different video.

So next time you hear "you just fit the known facts," here's the question to ask back. [pause] *Could I have?* If the theory is small enough that fitting one thing forces the others, then no, I couldn't have, and the fit itself is the evidence.

Old facts always counted. We just told ourselves they didn't, because a new prediction was easier to trust. But the real test was never *when* you learned the fact. [pause] It was whether the theory had enough room to cheat.

And a small one doesn't.
