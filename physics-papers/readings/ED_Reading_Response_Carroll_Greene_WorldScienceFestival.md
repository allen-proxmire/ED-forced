# The Reading They Didn't Reach For: A Response to Carroll and Greene on Quantum Foundations

**Author:** Allen Proxmire
**Series:** ED Readings (interpretation / positioning line)
**Date:** 2026-07-27
**In response to:** the Brian Greene and Sean Carroll conversation on the foundations of physics (World Science Festival). This is a reading, an interpretive response, not a refutation and not a derivation. It engages their public arguments in good faith, steelmans the view Carroll defends, and offers a third option their two hours did not put on the table. It is factual about the mathematics and honest about what Event Density does and does not establish (§7).

## 1. The option that wasn't on the table
Carroll and Greene spent two generous hours mapping the foundations of quantum mechanics, and one of the best things they did was insist that these are not mere "interpretations" but different *theories* that can share predictions while disagreeing about reality. They walked the live options: Copenhagen's external observer, Everett's Many-Worlds, de Broglie-Bohm's pilot wave, and Carroll's own austere program of starting from a bare quantum state in Hilbert space and letting spacetime emerge from it.

One option never came up. Not that the wavefunction is real and fundamental (Everett), nor that it is mere bookkeeping (Copenhagen), but a third thing: that unitary quantum mechanics is neither fundamental reality nor mere bookkeeping, but an *emergent, reversible, coarse-grained description* of a substrate whose primitive act is irreversible commitment. On that reading the Schrödinger equation is exactly right and exactly not fundamental, the way the heat equation is exactly right about a gas and exactly not its molecules. This note is that third option, and it turns out to speak directly to the two places the conversation was most honest about its own difficulties.

## 2. Where Carroll is exactly right
Any response worth reading has to first say what its target gets right, so: Carroll's central claim is correct on its own terms. Many-Worlds is the most austere quantum mechanics anyone has written, one law taken literally with nothing added. His sharpest line in the talk is that the worlds are not even the point; the theory is about "obeying the Schrödinger equation all the time." And he concedes, with unusual honesty, that at the level of postulates it is the simplest option while at the level of matching our experience it is the hardest. That concession is the whole ballgame, and I want to take it seriously rather than score points off it.

Because here is the thing about "obey the Schrödinger equation all the time": it is a bet that the Schrödinger equation is the *bottom*. Everything Many-Worlds spends its energy on downstream, the branches, the probabilities, the arrow, is the cost of that one bet. The third option is simply the other bet.

## 3. The disagreement is one bit
Strip both views to the frame and they agree on the most radical-sounding thing in the whole talk: spacetime is not fundamental, it is emergent. Carroll wants it to emerge from the structure of a vector in Hilbert space. Event Density wants it to emerge from a relational substrate of participating chains. They agree on the heresy and disagree about the floor beneath it.

- **Carroll's floor is reversible:** a universal wavefunction under unitary evolution is the whole of reality, and irreversibility, definiteness, and time's arrow are emergent appearances.
- **The other floor is irreversible:** commitment, the arrow, is primitive, and unitary evolution is the reversible description of what has not yet committed.

That is the entire disagreement, one bit. The rest of the talk's hard problems are what that bit costs on Carroll's side.

## 4. The seam the conversation exposed: probability
The most revealing stretch was on probability, and to his credit Carroll went straight at the hardest question: if every outcome happens, what could a probability even mean? His answer is subjectivist, self-locating uncertainty, two copies of you after a branching who don't yet know which branch they're on, and a claim that there is an obvious right way to assign their credences that lands on the Born rule.

This is exactly where I'd have wanted Greene to push, and he mostly let it pass. The difficulty is not subtle. If both branches are fully real, "how likely is spin-up" is a *counting* question, and counting equally-real branches gives even odds, not `|ψ|²`. To recover the Born weights you have to make a small-amplitude branch count for less while insisting it exists just as much as the other. Weighting equally-real worlds unequally is the one thing the ontology can't cleanly define, which is why the derivation has stayed contested for decades. The single fact that made anyone believe quantum mechanics is the least secure part of the most austere theory.

On the other bet the question dissolves rather than gets answered. There is one world; the alternatives do not happen. "How likely" is not "how many copies of me," it is "which commitment occurs," and the Born weight is a propensity of that single committal event. You are not dividing reality among branches, so there is nothing to miscount.

## 5. The crux, in one line: what happens to the coherences
Here is the disagreement made concrete enough to state without any metaphysics. Write the density matrix `ρ = ψψ*`. Its diagonal entries are the outcome probabilities; its off-diagonal entries carry the relative phases between outcomes, which is to say the coherence, the interference, the "quantumness." Ask one question: when a measurement happens, what becomes of those off-diagonal coherences?

- **Many-Worlds:** nothing removes them. Unitarity preserves them exactly and forever; they merely spread into the environment and become inaccessible. Every outcome stays real on its branch.
- **Event Density:** commitment genuinely diagonalizes `ρ`. The off-diagonals are removed, not hidden. One outcome becomes a fact; the others are gone. One world.

Same matrix, opposite fate for its off-diagonals. Everything else, the branching versus the collapse, the many worlds versus the one, follows from that. And it says where the two could in principle part ways empirically: any process that *genuinely* destroys coherence rather than dispersing it is impossible for Many-Worlds and native to Event Density. That is the objective-collapse and matter-wave-interferometry frontier, and it is the honest reason this view is a cousin of objective collapse, except that the collapse is grounded in a primitive instead of bolted on with free parameters.

(One factual point Carroll would be right to insist on, and I want to concede it before he does: this is about the *relative*-phase coherences, the off-diagonals. The *global* phase of a state is unobservable for a trivial reason that has nothing to do with measurement. Getting that distinction wrong is how these arguments go bad, so I'm flagging that I haven't.)

## 6. A tension worth naming
Early in the talk, Carroll is a thoroughgoing Humean about laws: laws don't govern or breathe fire into anything, they are compressed descriptions of patterns, and what exists is just the world doing its thing. I agree with him there. But by the quantum section he is a realist about the wavefunction, real, fundamental, the actual furniture of reality. Those two stances sit uneasily together. If laws are compressions we invent, why crown one particular equation as the literal fundamental stuff of the world?

The third option threads that needle in a way I think is more consistent than either of us has stated: be Humean about the *emergent* laws, the Schrödinger and Einstein equations are coarse-grained descriptions, shadows, and be realist about the *substrate* and its one primitive act, commitment. The fire is in the becoming, not in the equation. That is a single, coherent attitude toward what a law is, and it is not the one Many-Worlds ends up with.

## 7. Carroll's best card, and the honest ledger
Carroll's strongest general argument in the talk is historical: the recurring mistake in physics is losing the courage of your theories, failing to take your equations literally enough (his Kelvin's-paradox example is a good one). It is a real pattern and I won't wave it away.

But "take your theory literally" only points at Many-Worlds once you have already decided *which* theory is fundamental. Both of us agree you should take the fundamental theory dead seriously. We disagree about whether the fundamental theory is the reversible Schrödinger equation or the committing substrate underneath it. If it's the latter, then treating unitarity as the literal whole of reality isn't courage, it's insisting the smooth continuum is realer than the molecules. Which theory is fundamental is the whole question, and the courage argument quietly assumes its answer.

And the ledger has to stay honest, because this is a reading, not a proof. Event Density does not derive quantum mechanics from nothing. Its reproduction of the Born rule and the Hilbert-space scaffolding is postulate-conditional, and this response leans on none of that being finished. It leans only on the reading itself. The claim here is not that Many-Worlds is refuted; it is that Many-Worlds carries three costs, probability, preferred basis, and arrow, all traceable to one choice, and that the opposite choice does not carry them, at the price of a single primitive. That is exactly the kind of case Everett made in 1957. It deserves a hearing on the same terms.

## 8. The one-sentence version
Carroll and Greene asked whether the wavefunction is real or bookkeeping. The reading they didn't reach for is that it is neither, that unitary quantum mechanics is the reversible shadow of a substrate that commits, and that the moment you make commitment primitive, the three things Many-Worlds spends itself buying back, probability, a preferred basis, and the arrow of time, were never lost in the first place.
