# Many-Worlds, and the Door Nobody Opens
### A talking-head video script

**Author:** Allen Proxmire
**Series:** ED Readings — the spoken / popular endpoint (companion to the written standalone, `ED_Reading_ManyWorlds_TheOtherReadingOfSchrodinger.md`, which is the same argument at the physicist register).
**Format:** ~7 minutes spoken, first person, to camera. Written to be *read aloud*, not read. `[pause]` marks a beat. Delivery: unhurried, conversational, gracious. It steelmans Many-Worlds and never claims proof — that honesty is the whole credibility of it.
**Note:** the open names Carroll and Greene to ride a real conversation; cut the names if you want it evergreen.

---

I watched Brian Greene and Sean Carroll talk about quantum mechanics for two hours. Two of the best physicists alive, being honest and careful about the deepest question in the whole subject: what is actually real. And the entire time, I kept waiting for one door to get opened. It never did. So let me open it.

The idea they kept circling is called Many-Worlds. And if you've heard of it, you've probably heard the cartoon version: every time something quantum happens, the universe splits, and there's another you in another world where the coin landed the other way. It sounds like science fiction. But here's what you should know. Serious physicists take this seriously. Carroll is one of them. And they are not crazy to. [pause]

Here's why. Quantum mechanics has one equation at its heart. It works flawlessly. The trouble is what it says. Left alone, that equation does not describe one world with definite things happening. It describes every possibility at once, all of them real. To get back the single world you and I actually live in, the textbook quietly bolts on a second rule: when you look, all the possibilities but one vanish. And that second rule is ugly. It's vague. Nobody can even say what counts as "looking."

Everett's move, back in 1957, was beautiful in how simple it was. He said: stop adding the ugly rule. Just believe the equation. If it says all the possibilities are real, then they are all real. You don't see them because each one is its own world, and you only live in one. That's Many-Worlds. It isn't that Everett wanted a pile of universes. He just refused to add anything to the math. The worlds are the price of taking the equation completely literally.

I want to be fair to that, because it is the honest move. But it comes with a bill. [pause]

That equation runs exactly the same forwards and backwards in time. It has no arrow. It doesn't know the difference between the past and the future. But our world does. Eggs break, they don't unbreak. You remember yesterday and not tomorrow. So the moment you make that time-blind equation the whole of reality, you now owe an explanation for everything it left out. Where do definite outcomes come from? Where does the direction of time come from? And the hardest one of all: where does probability come from?

Sit with that last one. If every outcome actually happens, in some world, then what does it even mean to call a result unlikely? Every version happens, to some copy of you. There is a version of you reading this who is about to win the lottery. So what are the odds measuring? Many-Worlds has spent fifty years trying to answer that, and it is still arguing about it. The one experimental fact that made us believe quantum mechanics in the first place, the probabilities, is the shakiest plank in the theory that calls itself the simplest.

So here's the door nobody opened. [pause] What if that equation is not the bottom?

Think about the equation for a smooth flowing river. It is perfect. It is also not fundamental. Underneath it there are water molecules, discrete, bouncing, and the smoothness is just what they look like from far away. What if quantum mechanics is like that. Perfect, and not the bottom. The smooth, reversible surface of something underneath that is neither smooth nor reversible. Something whose most basic act is to commit. To make one thing actual, and let the others go.

Call that act the arrow of time. Not something you explain away at the end. Something you start with. [pause]

Now watch what happens, because this is the whole thing. This view and Many-Worlds agree on almost everything. They agree the math is right. They even agree that space itself is not fundamental. They disagree about exactly one thing, and I can say it in a single sentence. When you measure something, and one outcome happens, do the others still exist somewhere? Many-Worlds says yes. In the other branches. Forever. This view says no. They are gone. That is what it means for something to happen.

Same equation. Opposite answer to one question. That is the entire fight.

And look what you get for free on this side of it. Probability stops being a paradox, because there is one world and one thing happens, so the odds are just how often each outcome gets chosen. The definite result you see is not a mystery, it is the commitment itself. And the arrow of time is not a bill you pay at the end. It is the floor you were standing on the whole time.

Now I have to be honest with you, because this is exactly where most people in my position are not. I am not telling you this is proven. It is not. It is a reading of the equations, the same way Many-Worlds is a reading. Neither one is proven, and anybody who tells you their interpretation of quantum mechanics is settled fact is selling you something. What I am saying is narrower, and I think fairer. One of these readings needs an endless supply of invisible universes just to explain why a coin comes up heads. The other one needs a single thing: that time is real, and things actually happen.

And here is what lifts it above a debate. If commitment is a real, physical act, then there should be a size. A mass where it kicks in. Where a thing gets too big to be in two places at once, not because somebody looked at it, but because the universe committed. That is a number. And experiments with bigger and bigger molecules are climbing toward it right now. Many-Worlds says that boundary should not exist. This says it should. One day, one of them is going to be wrong. That is what makes it science and not a story.

So. The cartoon tells you the road not taken is a whole other world, with another you living it out. [pause] The other reading tells you something quieter, and I think braver. The road not taken is just gone. That is what it is for a thing to happen. The universe is not endlessly splitting behind your back. It is choosing. [pause] And so are you.
